Alex Schnell's agenda centers on what he calls pocketbook items — structural issues that determine how much genuine influence people have in public life. These are not abstract policy debates; they shape the relationship between citizens and their government and determine whether individuals can participate meaningfully in the political process. Fix the structure, and the rest of governance improves with it.

## Term limits

Long political careers weaken the connection between elected officials and the communities they serve. When a representative serves for decades, their obligations shift — away from constituents and toward party leadership, donors, and institutional self-preservation. Regular turnover strengthens accountability and brings new perspectives into the legislative process.

Alex supports the following limits:

- **House of Representatives:** No more than three terms (six years total)
- **Senate:** No more than two terms (twelve years total)

Alex also makes a personal commitment: he will serve no more than three terms in the House regardless of what Congress as a whole decides. A representative who believes in term limits should be willing to hold himself to that standard first.

## Transparency and accountability

Public trust depends on clear and accessible information. Many government records remain sealed for decades, including files related to major historical events and investigations. When the government withholds information from the people it serves, it weakens confidence in institutions and prevents meaningful oversight.

Alex supports:

- **Releasing long-sealed records** where appropriate, so the public can evaluate government actions and decisions that have been obscured for years
- **Strengthening protections for whistleblowers** who report waste, fraud, abuse, or illegal activity within federal agencies
- **Conducting regular audits of federal programs** to ensure public funds are being spent as authorized and that programs are achieving their stated purposes

Transparency is not a partisan value — it is the baseline condition for self-government. The public cannot hold officials accountable for actions it cannot see.

## Campaign funding and local influence

Campaign funding should reflect the interests of the people who live in the district a candidate seeks to represent. When contributions flow in from outside the jurisdiction — from corporations, political action committees, or wealthy individuals in other states — the candidate's attention follows the money rather than the community.

Alex supports a simple rule: **donations may only come from individuals who reside within the candidate's jurisdiction.**

The mechanism for enforcing this is straightforward:

- A **Social Security Number** is a valid donor identifier — it belongs to an individual person and can be verified as originating within the jurisdiction
- An **Employer Identification Number** identifies an organization, not a person, and should be excluded from campaign contributions entirely
- Both identifiers are invalid for contribution purposes if they originate outside the jurisdiction the candidate represents

Local voices should guide local representation. Large outside contributions shift a representative's attention away from community needs, reduce public trust, and make elected officials answerable to interests that have no stake in the lives of the people they are supposed to serve.

Government works best when it is open to the people it serves. Alex makes his legislative priorities public before taking office so constituents can see exactly how he intends to vote and lead.
