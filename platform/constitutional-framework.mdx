Alex Schnell approaches every vote and every bill through the lens of the Constitution. Congress must stay within the limits the Constitution provides — those limits are the foundation for a government that is accountable, affordable, and focused on its core responsibilities. The Constitution does not grant unlimited authority; it protects people from unaccountable power by keeping decisions close to the public and setting clear boundaries on what the federal government may do.

## James Madison on the founding design

James Madison described the constitutional framework plainly in Federalist No. 45:

> "The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite."

That principle guided the design of the American system of government and remains essential for effective self-government today. If Congress possessed general authority, the Framers would have had no reason to list specific powers at all.

## Article I, Section 8: The few and defined powers

Article I, Section 8 lists the powers of Congress. These are grants of authority — and also limits. Each power has a specific meaning rooted in the founding era. Below are all 18 enumerated powers alongside Alex's interpretation of their proper scope.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="1. Lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises">
    Congress may raise revenue for national purposes, applied uniformly across the United States. The spending must support the limited federal responsibilities listed in the Constitution — not serve as a general funding mechanism for programs untethered to any enumerated power.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="2. Borrow money on the credit of the United States">
    Congress may issue debt for legitimate federal functions. This clause does not authorize borrowing to fund programs that fall outside the enumerated powers.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="3. Regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Indian tribes">
    The founding understanding of "commerce" referred to trade and exchange — the handshake between parties across jurisdictional lines. It did not include manufacturing, agriculture, labor, or general economic activity inside a state. This power covers cross-border exchange; it does not reach production or consumption that remains entirely within one state.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="4. Establish uniform rules of naturalization and bankruptcy">
    These rules must be uniform across the entire country. This clause does not authorize broader control over immigration policy in general or over private financial management beyond the defined bankruptcy process.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="5. Coin money, regulate its value, and fix standards of weights and measures">
    This power ensures a stable national currency and consistent, reliable measurements for commerce. It is monetary and metrological in scope, not a license for broader economic management.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="6. Punish counterfeiting of U.S. securities and coin">
    This protects the integrity of the national currency. It is a narrow, specific criminal authority directly tied to the coinage power above.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="7. Establish post offices and post roads">
    This supports communication and national connectivity. It does not authorize general federal infrastructure programs, highway systems, or broadband initiatives that have no connection to postal operations.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="8. Promote the progress of science and useful arts through patents and copyrights">
    Congress may protect intellectual property for limited times to incentivize creation. This clause does not authorize federal control over research funding, public education, or industrial policy.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="9. Constitute tribunals inferior to the Supreme Court">
    Congress may create federal courts to adjudicate matters arising under federal law. This is an organizational power scoped to the federal judiciary, not a grant of jurisdiction over all legal disputes.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="10. Define and punish piracies, felonies on the high seas, and offenses against the law of nations">
    This covers crimes outside state jurisdiction and matters governed by international law. It is not a general federal criminal authority extending to conduct that occurs within the states.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="11. Declare war">
    The decision to enter armed conflict belongs to the people's elected representatives in Congress — not to the executive branch alone. This clause is a check on unilateral military action and must be honored as written.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="12. Raise and support armies, with appropriations limited to two years">
    Congress may fund land forces, but appropriations may not extend beyond two years. The two-year limit ensures civilian oversight and regular democratic review of military commitments.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="13. Provide and maintain a navy">
    Unlike armies, a navy may be maintained continuously given the nature of maritime defense. This clause supports national security at sea as an enduring federal responsibility.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="14. Make rules for the government and regulation of land and naval forces">
    Congress sets the standards and structure governing the conduct of the armed forces. This is the source of military law and the Uniform Code of Military Justice.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="15. Provide for calling forth the militia to execute laws, suppress insurrections, and repel invasions">
    Congress may authorize temporary federal use of state militias for specific, enumerated emergencies. This is a narrow emergency power, not a general authority to deploy domestic forces at will.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="16. Provide for organizing, arming, and disciplining the militia">
    Congress sets standards for militia readiness. The Constitution explicitly reserves the appointment of officers and the training of the militia under Congress's rules to the states themselves.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="17. Exercise exclusive legislation over the federal district and federal enclaves">
    Congress governs the seat of the federal government (Washington, D.C.) and properties ceded to federal jurisdiction, such as military bases and federal facilities. This authority does not extend beyond those specific locations.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="18. Make all laws necessary and proper for carrying out these powers">
    The Necessary and Proper Clause empowers Congress to choose reasonable means to execute its listed powers. It is a support clause — it extends the reach of the enumerated powers already granted, not a freestanding license to legislate on any subject Congress finds convenient.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Restoring the enumerated standard

Many modern federal programs operate outside the boundaries Article I, Section 8 establishes. Politicians have reinterpreted three clauses in particular — General Welfare, Commerce, and Necessary and Proper — to justify authority that was never explicitly granted.

These expanded powers are created by stretching the text rather than honoring its limits. The existence of an enumerated list itself is decisive: if Congress possessed general authority, the Framers would have had no reason to enumerate specific powers at all. When legislation relies on implied authority instead of enumerated power, the oath to uphold the Constitution is no longer being honored.

The Department of Education offers a clear example: it issues nationwide rules for local schools even though education is not an enumerated federal power. The 10th Amendment is unambiguous — powers not delegated to the federal government belong to the states or to the people. States and communities already manage education, environmental protection, healthcare access, and infrastructure with more flexibility and closer accountability than a distant federal agency can provide.

## Three proposed structural reforms

Restoring constitutional self-government requires structural changes to how candidates are selected and how campaigns are funded. Alex supports the following reforms:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Proportional electoral vote allocation">
    Return to the original proportional allocation of electoral votes that existed before the modern winner-take-all system. Proportional allocation reflects voter preferences more accurately across each state and works in harmony with the Electoral College as designed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Open primaries">
    Implement open primaries so that every voter within a jurisdiction can participate in selecting candidates. Candidate selection belongs to the people — not to party organizations that operate as private clubs deciding in advance who appears on the ballot.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Local-only campaign contributions">
    Limit campaign contributions to individual residents within the candidate's jurisdiction. Contributions from corporations, outside organizations, and individuals living outside the district should be prohibited. A Social Security Number serves as a valid identifier for an individual donor; an Employer Identification Number identifies an organization and should be excluded. Both identifiers are invalid if they originate outside the jurisdiction.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
**What this means in practice**

- Alex reads every bill through the lens of Article I, Section 8
- He opposes federal programs that belong at the state or local level
- He supports efforts to review, repeal, or return programs to states and local communities when they fall outside the enumerated powers
- He supports reforms that increase transparency, accountability, and fiscal discipline
- Respecting constitutional limits produces responsible budgeting, clear national priorities, and stronger communities with decision-making closer to the people
</Note>
