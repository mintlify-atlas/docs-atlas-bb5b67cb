Pennsylvania's ballot access laws create one of the most consequential structural decisions any grassroots candidate faces: which party line to run on. For Alex Schnell, that decision came down to practical math and state statute — not ideology. Understanding why requires understanding how the rules work and what actually happened when he tried to navigate them.

## Pennsylvania ballot access law

Under Pennsylvania Statutes Title 25 P.S. § 2911, the signature requirements for getting on a primary ballot differ sharply depending on party affiliation:

| Candidate type | Signatures required |
|---|---|
| Major party candidate | 1,000 signatures |
| Independent / minor party | 2% of the previous vote (typically 3,000–5,000+) |

<Warning>
Pennsylvania's ballot access threshold for independent and minor-party candidates effectively requires three to five times more signatures than a major-party candidate — gathered in the same short window, without party infrastructure, and with no support from established committee networks. This structural barrier makes independent runs extremely difficult for candidates without significant outside funding.
</Warning>

## Why the Republican ballot line

Alex is running on the Republican ballot line as a direct result of these legal requirements:

> "Pennsylvania's ballot access laws create significant barriers for grassroots and independent candidates. Collecting signatures must happen within a short window, and candidates outside the major parties often face higher thresholds without organizational support. To run a viable, community-focused campaign and meet these legal requirements, I am running on the Republican ballot line in this election cycle."

This is a practical choice for ballot access and voter outreach in PA-3, not an endorsement of party machinery or a commitment to party loyalty. As Alex puts it:

> "My approach remains independent in spirit and in practice. My commitment is to the people of the district and to the principles that guide my work, not to party leadership or political machines."

## The gauntlet: what signature gathering actually looks like

Even with the lower 1,000-signature threshold of a major party line, the signature-gathering window exposed how little organizational support Alex received from the Republican Party structure that had officially endorsed him.

The numbers from Alex's 2024 signature drive tell the story directly:

- **Total signatures gathered:** 266
- **Ward 9 contribution:** 119 signatures — **44.7%** of the campaign total
- **Alex personally:** 46 signatures — **17.3%** of the campaign total
- **Remaining 20 wards combined:** 147 signatures — an average of **7.35 per ward**
- **Campaign productivity vs. 1,000-signature goal:** **26.6%**

Alex individually out-produced the average ward output by more than six times, effectively replacing the work of six committee members just to keep the campaign alive. A campaign backed by twenty-one wards was performing at the level of roughly five fully functioning ones.

<Accordion title="What does 26.6% productivity mean in practice?">
At the rate the campaign was gathering signatures across all twenty-one wards, the organization would have needed to continue at that pace far beyond the legal window to reach the 1,000-signature threshold. The math revealed that the vast majority of the party's ward infrastructure was not participating in any meaningful way — leaving Alex and Ward 9 to carry the campaign essentially alone.
</Accordion>

## The "bonus" mentality: institutional indifference

When Alex raised the issue of missing support with party leadership, the response was revealing:

> "I was told that any signatures gathered by ward leaders were simply a 'bonus' and that I was expected to produce hundreds more on my own."

Three ward leaders showed integrity by organizing events. Alex's own ward leader and committee members personally knocked on doors and delivered nearly half of the campaign's total progress. But seventeen other wards remained effectively inactive during the signature window — some of whose leaders were reportedly vacationing during the most critical week of the drive.

> "In any other professional organization, a logistics department treating the delivery of a product as an optional favor would be grounds for termination; in politics, it is a gatekeeping tactic."

The endorsement gave Alex the party's name. The party withheld the infrastructure that was supposed to come with it. This "bonus" mentality — treating candidate support as optional rather than as the core function of party organization — is, in Alex's view, proof that the structure exists to serve the gatekeepers, not the candidate or the voters.

## Independence in practice

Running on the Republican ballot line does not change Alex's commitments. His platform, his funding model (no PAC money, no contributions from outside the district), and his approach to representation remain the same regardless of which line his name appears on. The party line is a legal mechanism. The campaign belongs to PA-3.
